Combined cycle power plants are leaders in modern power generation due to their superior efficiency compared to other cycles. This advantage lies in the clever marriage of a gas turbine cycle. But the key to unlocking even greater efficiency lies in supercharging the Rankine steam cycle with exhaust at the highest temperature. However, this is possible if we push the boundaries of operating temperatures, focusing on the inlet temperature of the gas turbine as it is the most critical point of the plant. Consequently, the development of gas turbines capable of operating at elevated inlet temperatures becomes crucial for achieving even greater efficiency in combined cycle plants.
This thesis takes the 1700℃ Class Ultra High Temperature Gas Turbine developed by Mitsubishi Heavy Industries (MHI) as a starting point. Through a technical review, I show the key technologies that have led to MHI's success. My focus is on two crucial areas: the novel Ni-based superalloy they developed and the techniques they employ to predict defects during the casting process.
Obviously, the superalloy they developed is currently under patent and for this reason I decided to take a general look on the evolution of the Ni-base superalloys by introducing the basic knowledge about the microstructure and the mechanisms that justify their strength and by giving an overview of the most common alloying elements used.
While the specific composition of the MGA1700 alloy remains confidential, this thesis explores the development flow that led to its creation, and it examines the preliminary tests conducted to evaluate the target properties of the superalloy.
Finally, this thesis explores the main defects of this superalloy explaining the techniques implemented by Mitsubishi to evaluate and predict them. By examining a specific defect, it is possible to gain valuable insight into the organization of the casting process. This analysis will also showcase a solution employed to achieve the single crystal structure of the superalloy.
